> The HDB concessionary loan has sat at 2.6 per cent since 1999. Singapore bank
> rates ran well below that for most of the 2010s, then rose above it in 2022 and
> 2023. By loan start year, the gap was not close.

The HDB concessionary rate is pegged 0.1 percentage points above the CPF Ordinary
Account rate, and the Ordinary Account has sat at its legislated 2.5 per cent floor
in every quarter a primary CPF Board document publishes, from 1999 to 2026. The
HDB rate has therefore been 2.6 per cent, flat, for the entire period this piece
covers.

Compounded 3-month SORA, the public benchmark Singapore mortgages have priced off
since 2020, moved a great deal over the same window: far below 2.6 per cent for
roughly a decade, sharply above it through 2022 and 2023, then back below it by
2026. A borrower who took the HDB loan in, say, 2011 was paying above the
benchmark for years before the benchmark ever caught up.

No Singapore authority publishes what a bank actually charged on top of that
benchmark, so this piece solves for it instead of assuming it, cohort by cohort,
and tests four predictions fixed in advance of seeing any rate data. Three failed.
This is a historical comparison by loan start year, past tense and cohort-level
throughout, and not advice: Jacob is not licensed under the Financial Advisers
Act, and none of this is tailored to any individual borrower's own loan.

## No published rate to compare against, so the margin is solved for

MAS's own Monthly Statistical Bulletin, Table III.3, lists eleven Singapore
interest rate series and not one of them is a bank's housing loan rate. The only
housing loan rate anywhere in the table belongs to finance companies, smaller
deposit-taking lenders funded more expensively than banks, and it runs only to
June 2022, before the rate rise the whole piece turns on. It is kept as a labelled
side comparison and never treated as a bank proxy.

So the design solves for the unknown instead of guessing it. For each loan start
year from 2010 to 2025, at a fixed S$400,000 principal over 25 years, the
calculation finds the constant margin over compounded 3-month SORA that would
have left a bank borrower's cumulative interest, from loan start to the last data
month, exactly level with an HDB borrower's on 2.6 per cent. That margin is the
realised break-even spread. It contains no assumption about future rates, because
it is measured only over months that actually happened.

{{< aside title="Why the reset interval is tested four ways" >}}
The base case reprices the bank loan to the benchmark every three years, "the
closest thing to a neutral assumption" rather than a fact about any specific
mortgage package, since no contractual reset schedule is publicly sourced either.
Every result in this piece is also recomputed at 24, 12 and 3-month resets, so a
reader can see how much of a finding depends on that one assumption and how much
does not.
{{< /aside >}}

## Early borrowers needed a startlingly generous bank

A loan that started in 2010 needed a bank charging between 1.89 and 2.05
percentage points over compounded 3-month SORA, depending on the reset interval
assumed, to come out level with the HDB route by the last data month. That is the
widest gap of any cohort at every one of the four reset intervals tested.

Every one of the six start years from 2010 to 2015 cleared 1.0 point at every
tested reset interval, without exception. The weakest of those six still needed a
margin of at least 1.43 points, rising to 1.64 points at the slower, 36-month
reset.

> **For 2010-2015 starts a pass is weak evidence and a fail is strong evidence,
> because the benchmark substitution favours a pass.**

That caveat is not decoration. Before 2020, Singapore mortgages priced off SIBOR,
not SORA, and SIBOR ran above SORA. Measuring the margin over SORA for those years
therefore overstates the margin a bank would actually have needed, which pushes
the result toward exactly what was found. A fail for those cohorts would have
survived that bias; a pass has to be read with it stated alongside, which is why
it travels with the number rather than sitting in a footnote.

By 2023 the picture had flipped entirely. That is the first start year whose
realised break-even spread came in negative at every tested reset interval,
ranging from -0.19 to -0.44 points depending on the reset. A negative spread means
the HDB loan beat compounded SORA itself, before any bank margin was added on top.

## Three predictions that did not survive the data

Four predictions beyond the headline were fixed before any rate data was opened.
One passed outright. Three did not, and none of the three is buried in a
footnote.

The prediction that every 2020-2023 start would flip to an HDB advantage within
three years, at every spread tested, failed. At the base 36-month reset, 15 of 24
start-year-by-spread cells missed that window, and 10 of those never crossed at
all inside the data available.

The prediction that refinancing opportunistically, rather than never refinancing,
would matter most for the earliest cohorts and least for the newest failed in the
opposite direction. The largest gap between the two rules sat with 2022-2025
starts, at 1.260 points, against 0.296 points for 2010-2015, the reverse of what
was predicted.

The prediction that switching from the HDB loan to a bank loan in 2021 would leave
every applicable cohort worse off than staying, at every spread tested, also
failed. Of 66 start-year-by-spread cases, 31 came in worse. Thirty-five did not.

## The borrower who switched and could not switch back

An HDB borrower can refinance to a bank loan; the reverse move is not available.
That asymmetry was modelled directly on a 2010 start, switching once in 2021 and
then repricing on schedule afterward with no further shopping around, the same
rule the 2021-switch prediction above was tested against.

At an assumed margin of 1.25 points over SORA, that switch cost $128,311 in
cumulative interest by the last data month, against $125,576 for staying on the
HDB loan throughout, a difference of $2,735. At an assumed margin of 0.25 points,
the identical switch in the identical year cost $116,183, some $9,393 less than
staying. Whether the 2021 switch helped or hurt the same borrower depended
entirely on the margin assumed, which is exactly why a prediction that it would
hurt everyone, at every margin, could not hold.

Switching earlier told a cleaner story. Every one of twelve cases where a 2010 or
2011 start switched in 2012 or 2015, at a margin of 0.75 points or below, came out
ahead of staying on the HDB loan.

{{< aside title="A finance-company rate that never fell below HDB, and stops in June 2022" >}}
The one published housing loan rate in Singapore, from finance companies rather
than banks, charged more than the HDB rate in every one of the 150 months it is
published inside the 2010-2025 window, without exception. It is an upper bound on
what a costlier class of lender charged, not an estimate of a bank rate, and the
series stops in June 2022, at the start of the period the whole piece turns on.
{{< /aside >}}

## What would prove this wrong

A published bank mortgage margin over SORA or SIBOR for 2010-2015, if one is ever
released, that came in below roughly 1.4 points would overturn the realised
advantage reported for those cohorts, because that is the floor every one of them
cleared here. No such figure exists in any source read for this piece; MAS's own
Financial Stability Review and its Information Paper on Residential Mortgage
Pricing were both checked specifically for one, in 2025 and 2021 respectively, and
neither publishes a level or a range.

A sourced official SIBOR history would also matter, though in a narrower way. It
would turn the direction of the SORA-for-SIBOR substitution into a measured size
rather than a stated bias, which could move the 2010-2015 range without reversing
the finding that the range clears 1.0.

## What this does not explain

No 2010-2025 start completes a full 25-year tenure inside the data available, so
every number here is realised to the last data month rather than a full-tenure
outcome, and no forward rate path is used to fill the gap.

What a bank actually charged is still not observable. The margin reported is
solved for, not read from a bill, because no Singapore authority publishes one.

Fixed-rate and teaser packages are approximated as a constant rate set at
inception, which is not how those packages actually behaved month to month, and
no primary source gives that history either.

Where any individual borrower's actual margin sat inside the range reported for
their cohort is not something this design can place. The range is reported per
cohort; a specific loan is not.

## What to take from this

The direction most people already expect held up: a loan fixed at 2.6 per cent
for decades looks worse the longer the market rate sat below it, and the earliest
cohorts carried the largest gap. What is less expected is how large that gap
stayed even after allowing for the one bias that could have inflated it, and how
cleanly the picture reversed by 2023, when the same fixed rate started beating the
benchmark outright.

The three mechanics predictions failing matters more than any of them sounds on
its own. The crossover timing, the cohorts refinancing helped most, and whether a
2021 switch was a mistake for everyone all turned out to depend on assumptions the
public rate path alone does not settle, which is a different kind of finding than
getting a prediction right would have been.

Watch whether an official SIBOR history becomes public. It is the one open item
that could turn the SORA-for-SIBOR bias from a stated direction into a measured
number without changing which cohorts cleared the threshold.
