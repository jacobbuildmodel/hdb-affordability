> Resale flat prices have gone up. That is not in dispute and saying it proves
> nothing. Affordability is a ratio, and over the last twelve years the bottom of
> that ratio moved faster than the top.

Ask when flats became unaffordable and you will be told a story about the last five
years. It is a true story. The Resale Price Index bottomed in the second quarter of
2019 and has climbed by more than half since. Anyone who started looking in 2020
watched the thing they wanted move away from them.

But 2019 was the cheapest year in more than a decade, and choosing it as the
starting point decides the answer before any arithmetic happens. Prices had been
falling since 2013. A window that opens at a trough and closes near a peak shows a
sharp worsening whatever else is true.

So this piece runs from 2013, which was a peak, to 2025, the last complete year, and
measures one thing: the price of a constant-quality four-room resale flat against
what households earn. Because the same flat in different years is not the same thing
as whatever flats happened to sell that year, the composition of sales is corrected
for twice, by two methods that assume different things.

## Everyone knows prices went up. They did

Between 2013 and 2025, a constant-quality four-room resale flat rose **40.4 per
cent** in price. That is the year-on-year path from a hedonic regression on 121,765
four-room transactions, holding town, floor area, storey height, flat model and
remaining lease still. A fixed-2013-basket index, which assumes nothing about
functional form and simply follows the same cells over time, gives 37.9 per cent.
The uncorrected median gives 38.2 per cent.

Those three numbers sit within 2.5 percentage points of each other, which is close
enough to say prices rose by around 40 per cent and far enough apart that the
correction is doing contested work. Both corrected figures are published here for
that reason.

{{< aside title="Why the composition correction matters less than expected" >}}
The mix of flats sold changes every year. More Sengkang and Punggol, fewer mature
estates; newer leases, then ageing ones. A rising median price is partly a changing
basket rather than a rising price.

Restricting the whole analysis to four-room flats removes most of that drift before
any modelling starts, which is why the corrected and uncorrected numbers end up only
2.2 percentage points apart. The pre-registered threshold for "composition mattered"
was 3 points, so on the letter of the test it did not, quite.

The hedonic was also refitted with remaining lease as a piecewise-linear spline
rather than the pre-registered quadratic, because the quadratic came back with the
wrong curvature. The spline recovers the expected shape, where value decays much
faster below sixty years of remaining lease, and moves the headline from 40.4 to
40.6 per cent. The misspecification was real and the index was insulated from it.
{{< /aside >}}

## Incomes went up faster

Over the same period, median monthly household income from work, excluding the
employer's CPF contribution, rose **51.3 per cent**. That is the basis HDB itself
assesses income on, which is why it is the one used here.

Forty against fifty-one. The ratio fell.

A four-room flat cost **5.43 years** of median annual household income in 2013. It
cost **5.04 years** in 2025. In between it fell to **4.04 years** in 2019, which was
the cheapest it has been in the whole window.

![Years of income to buy a four-room flat](/figs/fig1_price_to_income.svg)

*Figure 1. A four-room flat cost 5.43 years of median household income in 2013, 4.04
in 2019, and 5.04 in 2025. Constant-quality price from the hedonic index, divided by
annual median monthly household income from work excluding employer CPF
contributions. Sources: HDB resale transactions and SingStat, both via data.gov.sg.*

Run the same measure from 2017 and it rises by 0.76 years, which is a clear
worsening. Run it from 2013 and it falls by 0.39 years. Same data, same method, same
flat. The base year decides the sign.

## So why does it feel worse?

Because almost nobody is comparing today with 2013. They are comparing it with
2019, or with what a cousin paid in 2019, and against that year the deterioration is
real and large. The 2019 buyer got the best deal available in twelve years. That is
a fact about 2019, not a fact about now.

There is also a second thing happening, and it is not visible in the price at all.

## The grant went up and stopped working

Singapore is unusual in that the cost of borrowing has not moved. The HDB
concessionary loan rate is pegged at 0.1 percentage points above the CPF Ordinary
Account rate, and the Ordinary Account has sat at its legislated 2.5 per cent floor
throughout. CPF announcements ten years apart, for the first quarter of 2016 and the
third quarter of 2026, both state the same two numbers: 2.5 per cent, and an HDB
rate of 2.6 per cent.

Elsewhere, falling mortgage rates have repeatedly rescued affordability measures
while prices climbed. Here that rescue was never available. Which leaves grants as
the only lever that moved, and grants are means-tested.

For a buyer at the 20th percentile of household income, total grants rose from
105,000 dollars in 2019 to 145,000 in 2025. Over the same period the price of the
flat rose by 235,857 dollars. The grant increase covered **17 per cent** of the price
increase.

And for that buyer the mortgage servicing ratio, the share of monthly income going
to the loan, rose from 37.8 per cent to 47.1 per cent. For the median buyer it rose
1.8 points. For a buyer at the 80th percentile, 0.6 points.

I expected the opposite. The pre-registered test said the low-income buyer's change
would be at least 5 points smaller than the high-income buyer's, because that is
what means-testing is for. It is 8.8 points larger. The test failed, and the reason
it failed is the most interesting thing here.

![Which constraint sets the loan](/figs/fig3_binding_constraint.svg)

*Figure 3. Grants set the loan in 2019. The loan-to-value cap sets it by 2025. For a
buyer at the 20th percentile the loan is the lower of the two lines, and they swap
over, which is why a larger grant stopped reducing the monthly repayment.*

The loan is the smaller of two things: the loan-to-value cap, and the price less
whatever grants the buyer receives. In 2019 the cap was 90 per cent of the price, and
for a low-income buyer with a large grant the second number was smaller. The grant
set the size of the loan directly.

The cap was then cut three times, to 85 per cent in December 2021, 80 in September
2022, and 75 in August 2024. By 2025 the cap is the binding constraint. An extra
dollar of grant no longer reduces the loan at all. It reduces the deposit instead,
and for that buyer grants had already covered the deposit in full. The grant went up
and the thing it was doing stopped.

This is a correlation between two policy changes and one outcome, not a causal
estimate. Both levers moved at once and nothing here separates them.

{{< aside title="A caution about comparing the three buyers" >}}
The 20th-percentile buyer starts at a mortgage servicing ratio of 37.8 per cent and
the 80th-percentile buyer at 10.2. The same proportional worsening therefore produces
a much larger change in percentage points for the poorer buyer. In proportional terms
the ordering is the same and the gap is narrower: up 24.6 per cent, 10.2 per cent and
4.9 per cent for the 20th, 50th and 80th percentiles.

Both framings are in the repository. Neither rescues the pre-registered prediction.
{{< /aside >}}

## The middle aged out of the grant

The Enhanced CPF Housing Grant carries a household income ceiling of 9,000 dollars a
month. It was set at 9,000 when the grant was introduced in September 2019 and it is
still 9,000 today. Over those seven years the maximum grant rose from 80,000 dollars
to 120,000, and the separate ceiling governing flat purchase and the CPF Housing
Grant moved from 12,000 to 14,000 and then to 16,000.

Median household income crossed 9,000 in **2022**.

![Median income against the frozen grant ceiling](/figs/fig2_ehg_crossing.svg)

*Figure 2. Median household income passed the Enhanced CPF Housing Grant ceiling in
2022. The ceiling has been 9,000 dollars a month since the grant was introduced in
September 2019. Income here is an upper bound on HDB's assessed basis, which excludes
bonuses, so the crossing is 2022 or later.*

In dollars, the median household's Enhanced CPF Housing Grant went from 10,000 in
2019 to zero from 2023. A threshold that does not move admits fewer households every
year, and nobody has to change anything for that to happen.

The other half of that sentence matters just as much. The median household's **total**
grant still rose, from 60,000 dollars to 80,000, because the CPF Housing Grant for
resale flats went up by 30,000 at Budget 2023 and that grant is not tapered by income.
The middle lost the means-tested grant and gained more from the flat one. Quoting
either half alone would mislead.

## What would have to be true for me to be wrong

The 2013 starting point would have to be the wrong peak. If the right comparison is
with some earlier year when the ratio was lower than 5.43, the fall I report is an
artefact of where I started, exactly as the 2017 comparison is. The transaction data
is on a consistent registration-date basis only from March 2012, so I cannot test
that here.

The two composition methods would have to diverge further. They differ by 2.5
percentage points, which sits between my pre-registered thresholds for agreement and
for failure. If a third method landed well outside both, no single constant-quality
number would be publishable.

SingStat's income series would have to be the wrong denominator. It covers resident
employed households, not flat buyers, who are younger and differently distributed.
It also includes one-twelfth of the annual bonus, and HDB's assessment excludes
bonuses, so it sits above the basis HDB actually uses. That is why the 2022 crossing
is stated as a bound rather than a date.

## What this does not explain

Why prices moved. Two rounds of cooling measures, a fifteen-month wait-out rule for
private owners and its removal, pandemic construction delays and the BTO supply cycle
all sit inside this window. They are confounds, they are not separated here, and this
piece makes no claim about what caused the price path.

What happened before 2019 to grants. The Enhanced CPF Housing Grant replaced the
Additional CPF Housing Grant in September 2019. HDB's own 2011 flyer confirms the
older grant applied to resale flats and was worth up to 40,000 dollars, but its income
ceiling could not be found in any primary document, so no pre-2019 grant is modelled.
The grant measures run from 2019 while the price measure runs from 2013.

Anyone who is not a first-timer family. Second-timers, singles and non-citizen
households face different grants or none. The Proximity Housing Grant is excluded
throughout because it depends on living near a parent rather than on income.

Bank-loan borrowers. Everything here assumes an HDB concessionary loan at 2.6 per
cent. A buyer on a bank loan faced a very different rate path after 2022.

## What to take from this

Watch the gap between income growth and price growth, not the price. Prices rose
40 per cent over twelve years and the ratio still fell, because incomes rose 51 per
cent. If income growth slows while prices hold, the ratio worsens with no headline
attached to it.

Watch the Enhanced CPF Housing Grant ceiling. It has been 9,000 dollars since 2019
while every other threshold in the system has moved. If it stays there, the share of
households receiving any means-tested housing grant keeps falling on its own. If it
moves, that is a policy change large enough to redo this analysis for.

And watch which constraint binds. A larger grant does nothing for a monthly repayment
once the loan-to-value cap is the thing setting the loan. That is not visible in the
grant amount, which is the number that gets announced.
